I · Storytelling

Designing a narrative game for playful subversion

The Deceiviest Social Game

A five-player storytelling game in which one player writes a secret story and three others quietly try to derail it.

Status
Completed 2022
Project type
Tabletop storytelling game
Role
Game designer and facilitator
Format
Five players
Materials
Image dice, flipbook, pens
Methods
Ludic design research, two playtest workshops, brainstorming sessions

The project

The Deceiviest is a five-player storytelling game about creating a story and then trying to derail it. The first player rolls image dice and writes a secret three-part sentence across a flipbook. Three deviators each draw one phrase, adding details that distract from and distort its meaning. The last player, the recounter, sees only the drawings and must rebuild the story. At the end, everyone reveals their part.

Significance

Much adult play is instrumentalized for learning or productivity. The game makes room for adult playfulness for its own sake, and tests the difference between play, the structure of rules, roles and turns, and playfulness, the mischief, exaggeration and surprise that animate it. Rather than a fixed story, it offers a narrative space that distributes authorship across players and creates conditions for ambiguity, trust and low-risk mischief.

How it was made

The design drew on ludic design research, the magic circle of play and spatial narrative theory, building a structure that constrains possibilities while keeping them open. Two playtest workshops of five participants, each followed by reflection on when people felt most playful and what held them back, shaped every iteration: roles were simplified, the point system removed and sentence construction streamlined to strengthen flow.

Outcome

Playtests showed that playfulness cannot be forced through mechanics alone; it depends on clarity, trust and permission. Subversion works best in a balanced system, where too much ambiguity confuses and too much structure suppresses play, and where people play matters as much as the rules. The result is a participatory narrative game that invites agency, mischief and connection without coercion.
