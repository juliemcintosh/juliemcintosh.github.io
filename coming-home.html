A wooden puzzle about home

Coming Home

A wooden puzzle drawn from a first visit to my member First Nation community. Each piece stands on its own as a prompt for a story, and together the pieces balance as one form.

Location
Manitoba, Canada; completed in Vancouver, BC
Context
Practice-based inquiry into process and making
Status
Completed 2022
Project type
Storytelling object, wooden puzzle
Role
Designer and maker
Materials
Aspen, acrylic paint, Dutch oil
Methods
Poetic observation (after Jane Fulton Suri), free-form writing and sketching, woodworking

The project

Coming Home is a wooden puzzle in two versions, one painted in acrylic and one stained with Dutch oil. Its pieces draw on the landscape, symbols and relationships of a Northern Cree community: the sun over a wide landscape, healing colours, medicine, the York boat and two figures who hold one another. Apart, the pieces become prompts for teachers, parents and Elders to share stories of home, nature, medicine, youth and love. Together, they balance as a single stacked form.

Significance

The research took place over Orange Shirt Day 2022, a day to remember the Indigenous children of Turtle Island who were taken from their homes or did not return. In a community where identity is carried strongly through symbols, from ribbon skirts to orange shirts, the puzzle asks how a younger generation can take part in conversations of identity and cultural pride through play, forming identities of their own that are aware of, yet distinct from, the children who were lost.

How it was made

I visited my member First Nation community for the first time with my sister in 2022. Rather than seeking answers or design ideas in the moment, I let moments of significance take hold. Photographs and conversations gathered around what drew attention: rivers in autumn leaves, old boats, wide landscapes of trees no taller than a two-storey house, lunch with community members and a women’s talking circle. Knowledge keepers showed us the old treaty grounds, and a local jeweller taught us to make earrings.

Poetic observation allows for a percolation period, when observations are left to settle. The ideation remained as open as the research: free-form writing, and sketches of objects and landscapes to find their hidden lines and shapes. Each piece was then shaped to stand for something on its own, and cut from 1.5-inch aspen on the bandsaw and scroll saw.

What the pieces hold

From the centre outward, a mother and child hold each other, the child is held by a York boat, the mother is wrapped by the sun and the sun is held by the wide sunset fading into twilight.

  • The sunThe rising and setting sun, since it touches so much of the community’s wide landscape.
  • The skyThe three purple shades of the sky are what a member of the talking circle described to me as healing colours, and they are the colour of my gifted ribbon skirt. They also stand for medicine, in the form of three braids, like the sweetgrass I was gifted.
  • The nurturing figureThis figure represents women, especially the women who shared in the talking circle. They offered knowledge as nourishing as the medicine in front of us. It can also be read as a male figure expressing affection and support towards a child, or an inner child.
  • The childThe child in all of us, as much the children in the community as those who left home. The figure reaches for support, knowing that care is not something that just happens to you, but something you seek out. I intentionally didn’t paint it a traditional orange, to encourage the idea that children today can develop an identity with an awareness of, but separate from, the children lost to colonialism.
  • The boatMy version of the York boat, fused with a modern fishing boat. Fishing brings life to the community: it’s the lifeline to the lake, a source of food and an excuse to gather. The boat acts as a vessel, carrying the child home.

Outcome

The result is a set of pieces that work as story prompts on their own, for non-linear storytelling with the puzzle’s colours and shapes. The project practised informed intuition: the designer as a vessel for expressing an environment, never claiming to speak on anyone’s behalf. The puzzle is specific to one place, yet its pieces invite anyone to tell their own story of home.
